At Colorado's Frisco Creek Wildlife Rehabilitation Center, orphaned bear cubs gather leaves and fir branches and dig dens up to 7 feet long, [Colorado Parks and Wildlife (CPW) says](https://coloradooutdoorsmag.com/2026/11/12/orphaned-burned-and-broken-the-frisco-creek-bears-of-2026/).

Their mothers aren't there to show them how.

The behavior offers a glimpse of how young bears prepare for winter on their own. A GPS tracking project will help researchers follow what happens when rehabilitated cubs leave the facility, which is [near Del Norte in the San Luis Valley](https://cpw.state.co.us/news/12022025/media-availability-bear-cubs-orphaned-last-summer-be-released-wild).

## Do bear cubs need their mothers to hibernate?

Orphaned cubs can prepare winter shelter without a mother's help. Two recent studies cited by Michael Sirochman, Frisco Creek's facility manager, found that cubs released before winter traveled seven days on average before finding a spot and making their own dens, [CPW reports](https://coloradooutdoorsmag.com/2026/11/12/orphaned-burned-and-broken-the-frisco-creek-bears-of-2026/). Frisco Creek staff see the same instinct before release.

But finding shelter is only one part of surviving outside rehabilitation. Researchers also need to know whether bears survive and stay out of conflict with people.

A study of 42 rehabilitated black bears, released in 2015 and 2016 in Great Smoky Mountains National Park and Cherokee National Forest in Tennessee and North Carolina, found their survival similar to or higher than published rates for wild bears, [according to the U.S. Geological Survey's summary](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70210925) of the study. Three of the 42 engaged in conflict behavior within a year of release; all three had been involved in conflict behavior with their mothers before they were orphaned.

Results differed by age. Estimated annual survival was 64 percent for the 13 bears released as cubs, while none of the 29 released as yearlings died within a year. The authors suggested releasing cubs in the fall, when wild food is plentiful.

Those findings cannot predict the outcome for Colorado's bears. They show why following animals after release matters: opening an enclosure is the start of their return to the wild, not proof that rehabilitation has worked.

## How Colorado prepares orphaned bear cubs for release

Frisco Creek took in 47 bears this year, a record for the center, which has averaged about 20 orphaned cubs a year over the past 15 years, [CPW says](https://coloradooutdoorsmag.com/2026/11/12/orphaned-burned-and-broken-the-frisco-creek-bears-of-2026/). Caretakers feed the bears and clean their enclosures behind visual barriers, so the bears don't see them.

Cubs that reach a minimum weight, 70 pounds for males and 60 for females, are released between mid-November and mid-December in fair weather and find their own dens. Others are placed in artificial dens, most often in February, or stay at the center until spring. Sirochman expects 31 of this year's cubs to go back to the wild this fall, mostly by the end of November, and about 16 to go to artificial dens or stay through the winter.

## What the GPS tracking study will measure

In its November 12 announcement, CPW said a Great Outdoors Colorado grant will put GPS collars on 10 cubs released from Frisco Creek this fall, so their movement and survival can be monitored for up to 18 months.

The data will help measure how well the cubs adapt and whether current release strategies work, CPW says. Mark Vieira, CPW's carnivore and furbearer program manager, called it a pilot study to learn whether monitoring orphaned cubs is practical. If cubs released straight to the wild in the fall survive the winter as well as those placed in artificial dens, he said, CPW could cut the habituation and costs that come with a longer stay at the center.

Until now, CPW has learned about released cubs mostly from ear tags on bears that turn up again. Vieira said that covers only a small share of the animals and says nothing of those that die without being found.
