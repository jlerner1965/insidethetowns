Manitou Springs grew into a resort town around its water: people came for the mineral springs and the mountain setting, and the lodgings and commercial buildings followed. The local museum is the place to start.

## Start with local interpretation

The [Manitou Springs Heritage Museum](/places/manitou-springs-heritage-center/) at 517 Manitou Avenue collects and presents the town's history, with exhibitions and talks that explain what is outside. Its calendar lists opening times and public programs; private events on it are not open to drop-in visitors.

## Read the springs as places

The [mineral springs listing](/places/manitou-mineral-springs/) points to the local spring guide and its map of the public sites. Some are small street-side stops; others sit beside a park or a historic building.

The storefronts and architecture on Manitou Avenue reward a short, slow walk. The [walking-order guide](/articles/explore-manitou-springs-in-walking-order/) sets out the route, with public spaces between the indoor visits. Crowds, crossings and parking favor a few chosen stops over trying to see everything.

## Choose a second interior if you want more

[Miramont Castle](/places/miramont-castle/) is a second museum in a different kind of historic building. Tours, admission and tea are arranged separately, and tea needs an advance reservation. [The Cliff House](/places/the-cliff-house-at-pikes-peak/), another historic building, is still a working hotel.

## Keep the town and the mountain distinct

The railway and the mountain trails draw visitors through Manitou but need their own reservations and access checks, and a history afternoon needs neither the Incline nor a summit trip. With children, pair the museum or a short spring walk with [Soda Springs Park](/places/soda-springs-park/); the [family guide](/articles/manitou-springs-with-kids/) has more.
